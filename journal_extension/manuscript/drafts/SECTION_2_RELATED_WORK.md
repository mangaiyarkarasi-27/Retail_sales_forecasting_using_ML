# SECTION 2: RELATED WORK

## 2.1 Retail Demand Forecasting

Retail demand forecasting has undergone significant methodological evolution over the past several decades, transitioning from classical univariate statistical modeling to high-dimensional machine learning frameworks \cite{mustapha2025forecasting}. Early supply chain literature established the mathematical foundations of demand estimation through linear time-series techniques, notably Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), Exponential Smoothing (ETS), and classical decomposition models \cite{syntetos2016supply}, \cite{yeesin2024application}. These traditional approaches rely on the premise that future demand patterns can be modeled as linear combinations of past observations and stationary stochastic disturbances. While computationally efficient and mathematically tractable, univariate statistical models operate on a series-by-series ("local") basis, fitting separate parameters to each individual SKU or store location in isolation \cite{mitra2022comparative}. Consequently, they are incapable of pooling cross-sectional information across heterogeneous retail portfolios, modeling complex non-linear feature interactions, or incorporating external variables such as price elasticities, promotional calendars, and macroeconomic indices \cite{venkiteela2025machine}, \cite{malik2024sales}.

In multi-channel retail environments, demand time series exhibit pronounced operational complexities, including dual seasonal cycles (day-of-week and month-of-year), intermittent zero-demand periods, promotional demand spikes, and brand-level cannibalization effects \cite{alghurabli2025ai}, \cite{tiwari2025agentic}. To address these non-linear dynamics, empirical forecasting research has increasingly shifted toward "global" forecasting architectures, where a single predictive model is trained simultaneously across hundreds or thousands of related time series \cite{makridakis2022m5}. Global models leverage shared parameter representations, effectively learning common seasonal harmonics and category-level trends from high-volume products to improve the estimation of volatile, lower-volume items \cite{hobor2026comparative}. Furthermore, retail planning necessitates multi-step-ahead forecasts over replenishment horizons $h \in \{1, \dots, H\}$ matching supplier lead times. While classical methods generate multi-step forecasts via recursive autoregressive iteration, recent literature emphasizes the importance of direct multi-horizon modeling to avoid compounding autoregressive errors \cite{tiwari2026explainable}. 

*Summary & Limitation*: While existing retail forecasting literature has firmly established the statistical superiority of global cross-series modeling over localized univariate methods, the vast majority of retail studies evaluate models solely on single-step or recursive point-accuracy metrics, frequently overlooking the operational causality of multi-horizon feature construction.

## 2.2 Machine Learning and Gradient-Boosted Retail Forecasting

The advent of competitive forecasting benchmarks, particularly the Walmart M5 competition, catalyzed a major paradigm shift toward Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDTs) as the dominant architecture for structured retail sales data \cite{makridakis2022m5}. Algorithms such as Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) \cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM, and CatBoost have consistently outperformed both classical statistical models and complex deep neural networks (e.g., LSTM, DeepAR, Temporal Fusion Transformers) on tabular demand datasets \cite{hobor2026comparative}, \cite{swami2020predicting}. The empirical dominance of GBDTs in retail stems from their innate ability to handle mixed categorical and continuous variables, model sharp non-linear threshold effects (e.g., weekend versus weekday surges), maintain robustness against missing values and extreme outliers, and operate with substantially lower computational training latency than recurrent neural architectures \cite{ogunseye2024predicting}, \cite{malik2024sales}.

Feature engineering represents the critical performance determinant in GBDT-based retail forecasting. Standard practices involve extracting three primary feature classes: (1) historical autoregressive lag observations (e.g., sales at $t-1, t-7, t-14, t-30$), (2) rolling window summary statistics (e.g., 7-day and 30-day moving averages and standard deviations), and (3) deterministic calendar indicators (e.g., day of week, month, week of year, quarter, and month-boundary indicators) \cite{mitra2022comparative}, \cite{alghurabli2025ai}. However, an unresolved methodological vulnerability in many applied GBDT studies involves feature temporal alignment during multi-horizon forecasting. In standard recursive pipelines, models utilize 1-day lagged demand ($y_{t-1}$) to predict $y_t$, and subsequently substitute predicted values as inputs for subsequent horizons ($t+1, t+2$). This practice risks severe error compounding and, in poorly controlled feature pipelines, can inadvertently introduce look-ahead feature leakage if features access ground-truth sales within the forward forecast origin window \cite{tiwari2026explainable}. 

*Summary & Limitation*: Prior research demonstrates that feature-engineered GBDTs provide state-of-the-art retail point predictions. However, existing implementations predominantly rely on recursive single-step architectures or do not enforce strict decision-time lag constraints across multi-day lead-time horizons.

## 2.3 Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) in Retail Decision Support

Despite their high predictive accuracy, advanced machine learning models operate as complex "black boxes," creating substantial barriers to organizational trust, auditing, and adoption in supply chain planning \cite{lin2026critical}, \cite{chaganti2026advancing}. In enterprise retail workflows, human inventory planners frequently override automated machine learning forecasts in favor of subjective heuristics when they cannot understand the underlying drivers of a prediction—a phenomenon known as the "explanation gap" \cite{narlawar2025explainable}, \cite{chang2025explainable}. To resolve this challenge, recent literature in Decision Support Systems (DSS) has integrated post-hoc Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, prominently Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) \cite{lundberg2017unified} and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), to provide mathematically grounded feature attribution scores \cite{tiwari2026explainable}, \cite{madabhushini2025explainable}.

In our foundational conference baseline study \cite{e_idss_ieee}, we developed the Explainable Intelligent Decision Support System (E-IDSS), integrating an optimized XGBoost forecasting model with TreeSHAP to generate both local (instance-level) and global (portfolio-level) feature importance attributions. TreeSHAP leverages cooperative game theory to allocate fair additive contributions to each feature, enabling store managers to verify whether a forecasted sales increase is driven by legitimate calendar seasonality, promotional lifts, or historical baseline momentum \cite{lundberg2017unified}, \cite{meshram2025explainable}. Furthermore, E-IDSS coupled these attributions with a Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) scoring engine to categorize products into descriptive priority tiers (e.g., "Increase Inventory" vs. "Reduce Procurement") \cite{e_idss_ieee}.

However, a fundamental epistemological distinction exists between model explainability, uncertainty quantification, and operational decision-making:
1. **Explainability** addresses a retrospective attribution question: *"Which input features contributed to the generation of this specific point forecast?"* \cite{lundberg2017unified}, \cite{hossain2026ethical}.
2. **Uncertainty Quantification** addresses a prospective probabilistic question: *"What is the potential dispersion and tail risk of actual future demand around this point forecast?"* \cite{gneiting2007strictly}.
3. **Inventory Decision Support** addresses a prescriptive operational question: *"What exact physical order quantity should be placed today to balance holding costs against stockout penalties over the replenishment lead time?"* \cite{bertsimas2020predictive}.

*Summary & Limitation*: While XAI successfully resolves the descriptive explanation gap by establishing why a model predicts a given demand value, it does not quantify forward predictive uncertainty or prescribe exact, risk-buffered replenishment order quantities.

## 2.4 Probabilistic Demand Forecasting and Uncertainty Quantification

Because deterministic point forecasts ($\hat{y}_{t+h}$) provide only conditional expected values without communicating distributional variance, the supply chain forecasting literature has increasingly emphasized probabilistic demand forecasting \cite{gneiting2007strictly}, \cite{huber2019integrating}. In supply chain planning, forecast uncertainty can be decomposed into two distinct components: (1) **aleatoric uncertainty**, which represents the inherent, irreducible stochastic variability of consumer purchasing behavior, and (2) **epistemic uncertainty**, which represents the predictive model's parameter and structural uncertainty stemming from finite training data or model mis-specification \cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}. Capturing both uncertainty forms is vital for sizing safety stock buffers capable of absorbing demand surges without inducing excessive overstocking \cite{silver1998inventory}.

Probabilistic forecasting methodologies fall broadly into parametric and non-parametric paradigms. Parametric approaches fit specific theoretical probability distributions (e.g., Gaussian, Negative Binomial, Gamma) to demand errors or directly parameterize deep probabilistic networks (e.g., DeepAR) \cite{salinas2020deepar}. However, parametric assumptions frequently fail in retail settings due to pronounced demand skewness, heavy tails, and zero-inflated intermittency \cite{syntetos2016supply}. Non-parametric approaches, such as Quantile Regression Gradient Boosting (GBDT with pinball loss) \cite{huber2019integrating}, estimate specific conditional quantiles without assuming distributional shapes. The statistical quality of probabilistic forecasts is formally evaluated using proper scoring rules, most notably the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) and the Winkler score (interval score) \cite{gneiting2007strictly}. The Winkler score provides a mathematically rigorous metric that simultaneously rewards narrow interval width while heavily penalizing ground-truth observations that breach upper or lower prediction bounds \cite{gneiting2007strictly}.

*Summary & Limitation*: Existing probabilistic forecasting literature demonstrates that non-parametric quantile estimation outperforms rigid Gaussian assumptions. However, standard quantile regression models do not provide finite-sample coverage guarantees and can suffer from quantile crossing and instability under out-of-sample distribution shifts.

## 2.5 Conformal Prediction for Time-Series Forecasting

To overcome the limitations of uncalibrated heuristic intervals, distribution-free **Conformal Prediction (CP)** has emerged as a premier framework for uncertainty quantification \cite{vovk2005algorithmic}, \cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}. Conformal prediction wraps around any black-box point forecasting algorithm—including gradient boosted decision trees—and utilizes historical calibration residuals to construct rigorous prediction intervals with guaranteed finite-sample marginal coverage \cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}. In standard split-conformal regression, a nonconformity score $s_i = |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$ measures the deviation between true outcomes and point predictions on an unseen calibration set. The $(1-\alpha)$ prediction interval for a new test instance is then formed by adding and subtracting the empirical $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n$ quantile of calibration scores to the point prediction \cite{vovk2005algorithmic}.

Extending conformal prediction to sequential time series introduces fundamental theoretical and methodological challenges \cite{stankeviciute2021conformal}. Classical conformal guarantees rely strictly on the mathematical assumption of **data exchangeability** (which is automatically satisfied if observations are identically and independently distributed, i.i.d.) \cite{vovk2005algorithmic}. However, retail time series inherently exhibit strong temporal autocorrelations, seasonality, and evolving macroeconomic regimes, violating exchangeability \cite{barber2023conformal}. To adapt conformal methods to sequential data, recent research has explored rolling-origin calibration pools, sequential online conformal prediction, and adaptive nonconformity weighting \cite{stankeviciute2021conformal}, \cite{gibbs2021adaptive}. Furthermore, in multi-item retail portfolios, residual variance is strongly heteroskedastic, scaling proportionally with forecasted sales volume \cite{tibshirani2019conformal}. Scale-normalized or forecast-conditional nonconformity measures—where residuals are scaled by predicted magnitudes—have been proposed to produce locally adaptive interval widths \cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}.

*Summary & Limitation*: Conformal prediction provides a robust, distribution-free foundation for constructing prediction intervals. However, standard formulations assume exchangeability, and comparatively limited empirical research investigates how scale-normalized conformal calibration behaves across multi-horizon retail lead-time vectors.

## 2.6 Distribution Shift and Nonstationarity in Retail Time Series

A central challenge in real-world retail forecasting is **temporal distribution shift**, where the statistical data-generating process evolves between the training/calibration period and the forward operational test horizon \cite{barber2023conformal}, \cite{tibshirani2019conformal}. Distribution shift in retail demand manifests across multiple dimensions: (1) **covariate shift**, where the distribution of input features $P(X)$ changes over time while the conditional demand distribution $P(Y|X)$ remains stable, (2) **concept drift**, where the relationship $P(Y|X)$ shifts due to structural changes in consumer behavior, and (3) **residual scale expansion**, where unmodeled forward market volatility causes the dispersion of forward forecast errors to expand dramatically relative to historical calibration residuals \cite{barber2023conformal}.

When nonstationary retail series undergo substantial variance expansion, the exchangeability assumption underlying conformal prediction breaks down \cite{barber2023conformal}. Recent theoretical work by Barber et al. \cite{barber2023conformal} and Tibshirani et al. \cite{tibshirani2019conformal} proved that when test distributions drift from calibration distributions (measured under total variation distance), empirical coverage degrades proportionally to the magnitude of the distribution shift. Consequently, a conformal calibration layer that achieves perfect nominal coverage (e.g., 90%) on historical validation data may suffer significant empirical under-coverage (e.g., 20%–40% empirical coverage) when deployed across a forward test year undergoing macroeconomic scale expansion \cite{barber2023conformal}. In operations research, failing to diagnose and account for distribution shift can lead to severely undersized safety stock buffers and widespread inventory stockouts \cite{syntetos2016supply}.

*Summary & Limitation*: While theoretical literature establishes that distribution shift causes conformal coverage degradation, empirical retail studies rarely perform exhaustive location-scale diagnostic decompositions to quantify how temporal variance expansion affects multi-horizon prediction intervals and downstream supply chain decisions.

## 2.7 Inventory Replenishment and Safety Stock Formulations

In supply chain operations, inventory control systems manage the physical flow of goods to meet customer demand while minimizing total logistics costs \cite{silver1998inventory}, \cite{axsaeter2015inventory}. In continuous-review $(r, Q)$ and $(s, S)$ inventory control policies, the inventory position $IP_t = OH_t + OO_t$ (defined as on-hand inventory $OH_t$ plus on-order pipeline inventory $OO_t$) is continuously monitored. Whenever $IP_t$ drops to or below the Reorder Point ($ROP_{t,L}$), a replenishment order of quantity $Q_t = ROP_{t,L} - IP_t$ is placed with an upstream supplier \cite{axsaeter2015inventory}, \cite{snyder2019fundamentals}. Over a deterministic supplier delivery lead time of $L$ days, the reorder point must satisfy:
$$ROP_{t,L} = \hat{D}_{t,L} + SS_{t,L}$$
where $\hat{D}_{t,L} = \sum_{k=1}^L \hat{y}_{t+k}$ represents the cumulative point forecast of demand over lead time $L$, and $SS_{t,L}$ represents the safety stock buffer \cite{silver1998inventory}.

Classical inventory theory sizes safety stock using parametric Gaussian formulations: $SS_{t,L} = z_{\alpha} \cdot \sigma_L$, where $z_{\alpha}$ is the standard normal inverse cumulative distribution function corresponding to a target cycle service level $\alpha$, and $\sigma_L = \sqrt{L} \cdot \sigma_{\text{daily}}$ represents the standard deviation of lead-time demand under i.i.d. assumptions \cite{silver1998inventory}, \cite{axsaeter2015inventory}. However, in real-world retail, the classical parametric formula suffers from three severe operational failures:
1. **Gaussian Mis-specification**: Retail sales distributions exhibit heavy right tails and skewness, causing parametric formulas to systematically underestimate required safety stock during peak demand surges \cite{syntetos2016supply}.
2. **Independence Violation**: Daily forecast errors over multi-day lead times ($L=3, 5, 7$) exhibit strong temporal autocorrelations, rendering the theoretical $\sqrt{L}$ scaling rule invalid \cite{snyder2019fundamentals}.
3. **Lost Sales Dynamics**: In retail store operations, unmet customer demand is rarely backordered; it is permanently lost \cite{silver1998inventory}. Lost sales dynamics introduce non-linear truncations in closing inventory ($OH_t^{\text{close}} = \max(0, OH_t^{\text{start}} - y_t)$), breaking linear inventory conservation assumptions.

*Summary & Limitation*: Traditional safety stock formulations rely on restrictive Gaussian and backorder assumptions that fail in retail environments. Non-parametric, multi-horizon uncertainty intervals offer a promising alternative for sizing dynamic safety stock, but require rigorous validation in discrete-event lost-sales simulation.

## 2.8 Forecasting-to-Inventory Integration and Prescriptive Analytics

To bridge the historical disconnect between demand forecasting and inventory optimization, recent research has advanced toward "predict-then-optimize" and prescriptive analytics frameworks \cite{bertsimas2020predictive}, \cite{ban2019big}. Rather than optimizing statistical accuracy in isolation, prescriptive analytics integrates machine learning predictions directly into mathematical decision models that minimize operational supply chain costs \cite{bertsimas2020predictive}. In a landmark study, Bertsimas and Kallus \cite{bertsimas2020predictive} formalized a non-parametric framework that leverages machine learning feature weights to solve data-driven stochastic optimization problems, proving asymptotic optimality under general conditional distributions. Similarly, Ban and Rudin \cite{ban2019big} formulated the "Big Data Newsvendor," using Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) to map high-dimensional feature spaces directly into single-period order quantities via asymmetric pinball loss.

In retail settings, Huber et al. \cite{huber2019integrating} demonstrated that training GBDTs under quantile pinball loss to directly predict target service levels yields lower stockouts and waste than sequential parametric estimation in perishable bakery replenishment. However, the existing forecast-to-decision literature exhibits major structural limitations. The vast majority of data-driven newsvendor models \cite{ban2019big}, \cite{bertsimas2020predictive} operate strictly in single-period, static decision settings ($L=0$ or $L=1$), ignoring multi-day pipeline tracking, continuous-review reorder points, order-placement fixed costs, and multi-horizon lead-time dynamics ($L=3, 5, 7$). Furthermore, these models rarely incorporate formal conformal calibration layers to provide bounded uncertainty guarantees or examine how empirical performance degrades under forward distribution shift across multi-item portfolios.

*Summary & Limitation*: Existing prescriptive inventory literature demonstrates the value of integrating machine learning with decision models, but remains predominantly confined to static, single-period newsvendor models without multi-day pipeline simulation, conformal calibration, or multi-lead-time sensitivity analysis.

## 2.9 Cost–Service Trade-Offs and Asymmetric Losses

A fundamental principle of supply chain management is that inventory replenishment represents a multi-criteria optimization problem governed by an inherent trade-off between customer service and financial holding costs \cite{silver1998inventory}, \cite{snyder2019fundamentals}. Supply chain managers must simultaneously optimize three competing financial components:
1. **Holding Cost ($C_{\text{hold}}$)**: The capital, warehousing, insurance, and obsolescence cost incurred by carrying unsold on-hand stock ($h \cdot OH_t^{\text{close}}$) \cite{silver1998inventory}.
2. **Ordering Cost ($C_{\text{order}}$)**: The fixed administrative and transportation overhead incurred each time a replenishment order is dispatched ($K_{\text{order}} \cdot \mathbb{I}(Q_t > 0)$) \cite{axsaeter2015inventory}.
3. **Shortage Penalty ($C_{\text{short}}$)**: The direct financial penalty, lost gross margin, and brand reputation loss incurred for each unit of unmet customer demand ($p \cdot \text{Shortage}_t$) \cite{spanos2022application}.

In modern retail analytics, evaluating inventory performance solely through statistical accuracy (MAE/RMSE) or single service metrics (Fill Rate) provides a distorted operational picture \cite{huber2019integrating}. Sizing large safety stock buffers unconditionally elevates customer fill rate and eliminates stockouts, but drastically inflates average inventory investment and daily holding costs. Conversely, operating under deterministic point forecasts ($SS=0$) minimizes holding costs but induces severe stockouts and massive shortage penalties \cite{ban2019big}. The true operational efficacy of an uncertainty-aware replenishment framework must therefore be evaluated along the **Cost-Service Efficient Frontier**—identifying the exact operating conditions (lead time $L$, shortage penalty $p$, and nominal coverage $\beta$) under which uncertainty-derived safety buffers achieve superior total cost efficiency relative to deterministic baselines.

*Summary & Limitation*: Prior studies frequently evaluate service levels or inventory costs in isolation, lacking comprehensive portfolio-level empirical evaluations that quantify the exact cost-service efficient frontier across varying lead times and asymmetric shortage penalty regimes.
